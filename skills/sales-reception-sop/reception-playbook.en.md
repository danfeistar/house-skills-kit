# Reception & Needs Discovery · Playbook (English Edition)

> **Scope.** This playbook covers first-visit reception only: the greeting, the walkthrough route, needs discovery, matching, and the hand-off to a colleague or the next stage. It deliberately does **not** contain value scripts, objection counters, or pricing/closing mechanics — those live in the sibling skills, and this playbook only decides *when to call them*:
>
> - Value statements (location / amenities / floor plan / competitor / brand) → `sales-talk-library`
> - Customer objections ("too expensive", "let me look around", …) → `sales-objection-handling`
> - Price calculation and closing → `sales-closing-sop`
> - Lead grading and long-cycle follow-up → `sales-lead-grading`
> - Loan eligibility and paperwork → `sales-mortgage-sop`
> - Channel attribution and duplicate-lead arbitration → `channel-collaboration-sop`
>
> No policy numbers, no legal conclusions: anything regulatory is checked against the current local announcement on the day it comes up.

> **A note for English readers.** This playbook encodes how a Chinese new-home sales office actually runs, and several mechanics have no clean Western equivalent. They are kept and explained inline: the **sales gallery** (案场, the dedicated sales center with its sand-table model and show flats), the **property consultant** (置业顾问, the salesperson who owns a customer from first visit to contract), **rotation** (轮接, the fixed order in which waiting consultants take the next walk-in — stealing a colleague's walk-in is a disciplinary matter), **visit-source attribution** (判客/来访途径, deciding which acquisition source gets credit for the customer — because commissioned channel agents, the resident-referral program 全民营销, and unaccompanied walk-ins each carry different commission rules, attribution disputes are money disputes), the **sales-control board** (销控表, the live board showing which units are genuinely still available), and **subscription registration** (认筹, a small refundable deposit paid pre-launch to secure launch-day priority). Where the Chinese original refers to internal line codes (L1-1, L1-3, …), this edition points to the actual skill names instead.

## Part 1 Preparation & greeting

### 1.1 Before the customer walks in

**Five checks, every shift:**

- **Rotation order and gaps**: know where you are in the queue and when your slot comes. Follow the rotation — no poaching a colleague's walk-in, no forgetting to log one you took.
- **On-site materials**: price list, sales-control board, floor plans, delivery standards, and current campaign wording must be the live versions. Anything outdated comes off the table — handing a customer a dead price list is the fastest way to lose the room.
- **Your own state**: phone silenced, desk clear, pricing tools and the registration form actually working.
- **Today's source-registration rules**: confirm at shift start how channel-accompanied visits, resident referrals, walk-ins, and repeat visits are being classified and logged *today*. Rules get adjusted; yesterday's memory is not today's rule.
- **History lookup ready**: be able to check within a minute whether this visitor has been in before, who served them, what source was logged, and what the customer said last time.

### 1.2 Visit-source attribution: check in the background, never in front of the customer

Attribution decides who gets paid. That is exactly why it must never be adjudicated publicly: no announcing a source verdict while the customer is standing there, and no interrupting a reception to sort out ownership. The consultant receives normally; attribution is confirmed naturally in conversation, while a colleague checks history, reconciles the channel report, and completes the registration in parallel.

What to verify for each source type:

- **Channel-accompanied visit** (渠道带访): the channel's name, whether the pre-visit report was filed and is still inside its validity window, which agent accompanied, and whether that agent's credentials are current. A customer saying "a friend brought me" is a lead, not a determination — the paperwork decides.
- **Resident referral** (全民营销): who referred, whether the referral was registered before the visit, the relationship between customer and referrer, and whether the campaign rules current at *visit time* cover this case.
- **Walk-in** (自然来访): learn naturally, during conversation, how they heard about the project and whether anyone invited or accompanied them. **The absence of an accompanying agent does not auto-classify the visit as a walk-in** — that assumption is how attribution disputes start.
- **Existing-customer referral** (老客转介绍): let the customer describe the referral relationship naturally; the colleague or back office then verifies the original customer, the referral registration, and the applicable rules. Where this overlaps with the resident-referral program, the sales office's own policy decides — not the consultant's preference.
- **Repeat visit** (二访/多访): pull the history *first* — previous sources, previous consultant, registration status, the customer's own words last time — then naturally revisit it during this reception. **Re-verify this visit; never carry the previous classification forward**, because sources change between visits and stale attribution is the other classic dispute.

**Working principles**: an unclear source gets marked "to be verified" — never re-classified to grab credit. The back-office registration, the channel report, and the sales office's rules are what decide, in that order. None of this happens audibly in front of the customer.

**Natural-conversation samples** (weave into the tour, do not open with them):

> "How did you first come across our project — found it online yourself, or did a friend or a consultant reach out? I ask so the materials I prepare for you actually match where you're starting from."

If they have been in before:

> "Last time you were mainly looking at ___ — shall we pick up from that floor plan, or has something changed since?"

A hand signal, a quiet internal message, or a short code word brings the colleague in to look things up — the words "whose customer is this" are never spoken within the customer's hearing.

### 1.3 The first sentence

**Recommended opener:**

> "Welcome to {{project_name}}. I'm {{consultant_name}}, your property consultant here — {{how_to_address}} works fine. Is this your first visit with us, or have you looked into the project before?"

**What this sentence must accomplish**: project welcome, consultant identity, and a first-visit / repeat-visit check. That is all. Budget, size, and purchase purpose come later, at natural points in the walkthrough.

**Failure warnings:**

- ❌ Asking about budget or floor-plan size before you have finished introducing yourself — the customer has not agreed to be interrogated yet
- ❌ Cramming the brand story, the district outlook, the floor plans, and the price into one opening breath — nothing lands
- ❌ Switching to investor framing because the visitor "looks like an investor" — you are now pitching a need you invented

## Part 2 The first-visit walkthrough

### 2.1 The default route

```
Greeting & consultant introduction
  ↓
Source attribution confirmed naturally during the tour;
colleague checks, reconciles and registers in the background
  ↓
Developer brand
  ↓
District planning & area context
  ↓
Project master plan, amenities & daily-life scenarios
  ↓
Floor plans & show flats
  ↓
Other content on demand: property management, construction
status, delivery, price, incentives
  ↓
Pricing hand-off & departure confirmation
```

**What each stage must do:**

1. **Developer brand**: establish basic trust in who is building this — the developer, its track record on *this kind of* project, and the delivery background. Route the value statements to the `sales-talk-library` **brand block**. Do not inflate the company and do not recite an award list nobody asked for.
2. **District planning & area context**: where this is, where the city is heading, what transport exists **today**. Route to the **location block**. Plans and current state are stated separately, always — a metro line on a planning poster is not a metro line.
3. **Project master plan & amenities**: back to the project itself — building layout, landscaping, retail, schools, medical, the daily walking loop. Route to the **amenities block**, and only use what the customer has already told you matters.
4. **Floor plans & show flats**: matched to the household structure and functional needs the customer has actually voiced — usable area, gifted space (赠送面积, space outside the titled area), daylight, circulation, and where delivery scope ends. Route to the **floor-plan block**.
5. **Other content on demand**: property management, construction progress, delivery, price, incentives — driven by the customer's attention, not read out as a checklist. Price talk moves to `sales-closing-sop`; delivery evidence routes back to the brand block.

**Principle**: this is the default order for a first visit, not a script to be survived. A visitor who says "I only care about total price" or a repeat visitor who wants one specific floor plan gets a compressed route that respects why they came — but the compression never includes interrupting the reception to publicly sort out attribution, and never includes marching through every display board just to finish the boards.

### 2.2 Ask during the walkthrough, not in the opener

Once the opener has confirmed identity and visit count, the discovery questions — purpose, household, commute, function, budget, decision makers, timing — belong at natural nodes of the tour.

**Where each lands:**

- After the brand or district section: *"Which parts of the project had you already looked into? What do you most want to focus on today?"*
- After district and master plan: *"Where do you both work day to day? Between commute and daily amenities, which one weighs more for you?"*
- During the floor-plan talk: *"You said you want more space — is it bedrooms that need to be wider, or the living/dining area and storage?"*
- After the show flats: *"Of the spaces we just walked, which is closest to the problem your household actually needs solved? Anything you flat-out couldn't live with?"*

**When the customer says "just looking"**, do not treat it as an opener to argue with. Acknowledge, and structure the tour instead:

> "That's fair — buying a home deserves a proper look first. I'll walk you through the brand, the area, the project, and the floor plans in order; wherever something catches your eye, we stop and go deep there, so I'm not dumping everything on you at once."

### 2.3 The sand-table in three moves

The sand-table (沙盘) is the scale model at the center of the gallery. Three moves, in order:

1. **Locate**: brand story first, then zoom from the city to the district to the project, so the customer always knows *where they are* in the story.
2. **Connect**: attach what the customer has said — household, commute, daily life — to the corresponding part of the model as you go. ("You mentioned one of you heads east for work and the other does the school run — so let's look at the transport that exists now, the amenities inside the compound, and the child's daily walking loop.")
3. **Verify**: invite the customer to check — the map, the public notice board, the planning documents, the physical site outside the window. Expectations are never presented as current state, and anything unverified is labeled as such out loud.

### 2.4 Handing off to pricing

Trigger: the customer asks "how much?", asks you to run the numbers, or a floor plan has actually been matched.

> "Absolutely — let's first pin down the one unit you're genuinely weighing, then run it at its real size, floor, and payment structure. I won't hand you a vague lump-sum number. Once it's on paper you can judge for yourself whether it sits inside your budget."

**Before entering `sales-closing-sop`, confirm at minimum**: the target floor plan / rough size, the payment boundary, and whether the customer is willing to discuss specific units. Pricing an unmatched unit produces a wrong quote or drags the customer into a negotiation they never entered.

## Part 3 The needs-discovery questioning tree

> The tree is not a questionnaire to be read aloud. Open each dimension broadly, verify with **one** concrete probe, and never re-interrogate what the customer has already made clear.

### 3.1 The seven dimensions

| Dimension | Open question | Verification probe | Output field |
|------|---------|---------|---------|
| Household structure | "Who will actually live in the home?" | "Will parents or children be staying long-term?" | Occupants, headcount, life stages |
| Purchase purpose | "What's the biggest problem this move should solve?" | "If you could only fix one thing, which one?" | Owner-occupy / trade-up / investment / wedding home — **record their words** |
| Location & commute | "Where do you live and work now?" | "What's the longest one-way commute you can actually live with?" | Workplaces, transport mode, acceptable radius |
| Living function | "What's most inconvenient about your current home?" | "How would the new home have to handle that for you to call it solved?" | Room count, storage, daylight, floor, orientation |
| Budget & payment | "How are you planning to structure the money side?" | "Is there a hard cap on total price or monthly payment?" | Price boundary, down payment, monthly payment, payment structure |
| Decision members | "Who makes a call like this together, in your family?" | "For whoever isn't here today — what do they care about most?" | Decision maker, influencers, absent-member concerns |
| Timing | "When would you want to move in, or complete the trade-up?" | "Is that date a must, or an ideal?" | Purchase point, move-in point, triggering event |

**Bridging into sensitive territory** (budget, income-adjacent questions):

> "I ask about the payment range not to audit your finances — it's so I don't burn your time on homes that were never in range. If an exact figure is awkward, a rough band works fine."

**When the customer declines to answer:**

> "No problem at all — I'll mark it 'to be confirmed' and we'll work with what you do want to compare."

And then actually mark it. An unanswered question is recorded as unanswered; it is never back-filled with a guess.

### 3.2 Three ways to probe without interrogating

- **Specify**: *"More space" — wider bedrooms, or a bigger living/dining area?*
- **Rank**: *"Daylight, floor, total price — you can only keep two as priorities. How do they order?"*
- **Counterfactual**: *"If the price were right but the floor wasn't ideal — is that an automatic no, or still worth comparing?"*

**The anti-interrogation rule**: for every two or three answers the customer gives, return one playback or one matched response, so they can see the information landed. A consultant who only asks and never reflects turns a conversation into a form-filling exercise, and customers shut down accordingly.

## Part 4 Customer profile & need confirmation

### 4.1 The five-layer profile

```
Confirmed facts    — stated by the customer, verifiable from materials
Customer's words   — their own expressions, kept verbatim
Hard constraints   — not met → the home is out, no talking around it
Flexible items     — comparable, adjustable via price or structure
Unconfirmed        — not yet asked, or declined — never inferred
```

**A worked record:**

- Confirmed facts: couple, owner-occupy, child aged 5, currently living in {{current_district}}
- Customer's words: *"done with living far out"*, *"the living room has to be spacious"*
- Hard constraints: total price within {{price_cap}}; at least {{min_bedrooms}} bedrooms
- Flexible items: floor is comparable; renovation timing negotiable
- Unconfirmed: down-payment source, the final decision maker, exact school-place requirements

The five layers exist to keep you honest under pressure: when a manager asks "why are we showing them this?", the answer points at the hard-constraints line, not at a feeling.

### 4.2 Playback confirmation

> "Let me play back what I've got: couple with a five-year-old, owner-occupy; commute and living-room size matter most; total price around {{price_cap}} or under; floor is flexible, but fronting a main road is out. Is that right? If it is, I'll filter on exactly these — anything that fails them, I won't waste your time with."

**After the playback, stop talking and listen for the correction.** If the customer says "not exactly", their corrected version becomes the profile — not your original read, no matter how confident it was. A profile the customer never confirmed is fiction with good formatting.

### 4.3 When the requirements contradict each other

The customer wants the lowest total price, the biggest area, *and* a close-in location:

> "All three are reasonable — but the market makes you order them. I'm not choosing for you; here are three framings: price-first, space-first, location-first. Which one is closest to what your household actually needs?"

Three concrete packages break the deadlock; repeating "so what's your budget?" a fourth time does not.

## Part 5 Matching & showing

### 5.1 Matching rules

1. Hard constraints first, flexible items second. A unit that fails a hard constraint is out — no script rescues it, and pretending otherwise just moves the collapse to the negotiation table.
2. Recommend at most **three** units at once, in three roles: best fit / best value / contrast option. Five units presented at once is how a customer remembers none.
3. Every recommended unit gets its reasoning **and its known weaknesses** stated. A pitch that is all upside trains the customer to discount everything you say next.
4. Any project number you are not sure of gets marked "to be verified" — an illustrative number that slips into a quote becomes your problem at the signing table.

**The matching script:**

> "Going by the priority order you set: A fits the price cap best; B has the better living room and daylight but runs a bit higher; C is the most convenient location, with less space. Not all three will suit you — let's walk them and let the trade-offs sort themselves out in front of you."

### 5.2 What to watch during the showing

- Which space the customer lingers in or returns to
- Who asks questions, who goes quiet, who is clearly the one who decides
- Every "if only it had…" — that sentence is a need arriving late
- The moment the talk shifts from judging the home to asking about payment, availability, delivery, or what the family would say — that shift is warmth, and it is timestamped
- What draws an explicit no, versus mere hesitation — silence is not consent, and hesitation is not rejection

Show-feedback notes record the customer's **own words**. "Customer seemed satisfied" is not a record; it is a mood.

### 5.3 Routing to the value-script library

| The customer is focused on | Call from `sales-talk-library` |
|---------|----------|
| Commute, district, planning | Location block |
| Schools, hospitals, shopping, daily life | Amenities block |
| Gifted space, usable area, daylight, circulation | Floor-plan block |
| Comparing two projects | Competitor block |
| Developer, property management, delivery | Brand block |

Reception decides *which block to call*; the block itself carries the wording. Writing a fifth variation of the location script inside a reception note is how one project ends up with six versions of the same pitch and no one knows which is current.

## Part 6 Team hand-off & exceptions

### 6.1 The first-visit hand-over summary

```
【Source】            walk-in / channel / referral / repeat visit ("to be verified" items listed separately)
【Customer's words】   at least 2 key sentences, verbatim
【Confirmed】          what is settled across household, purpose, location, function, budget, decision, timing
【Hard constraints】   conditions that fail → excluded
【Flexible items】     conditions open to comparison and adjustment
【Unconfirmed】        declined by the customer or pending verification
【Units seen】         which ones, and the feedback on each in the customer's words
【Skills triggered】   talk library / objection handling / pricing-closing (if any)
【Next step】          who does what, when, through which channel
【Do-not-do list】     what the customer clearly disliked or rejected
```

A colleague picking this up cold should know in thirty seconds what moved, what stalled, and what must not be repeated. If the summary needs a verbal addendum to make sense, the summary was not done.

### 6.2 Supervisor escalation

**Triggers**: visible family disagreement, a special payment arrangement, a project question the consultant cannot verify, a request beyond the consultant's authority, a channel-attribution dispute.

The consultant's bridge, in front of the customer:

> "This one touches {{approval_or_attribution}} — I'm not going to guess at it in front of you. Let me bring in my supervisor so you get the accurate version, not my best attempt."

After stepping in, the supervisor **listens to the whole question first**, then answers. Contradicting the consultant and then improvising a different unverified number, in front of the customer, converts one problem into two.

### 6.3 Exception scenes

- **Customer refuses to register**: explain what the registration is for, honestly. If they still refuse, log "registration declined" per the office's rules — no quietly keeping the details on a phone, no routing around the rule.
- **Repeat visit**: pull the history and the customer's own words *before* saying anything, so the second reception is not the first reception again. Ownership follows the established rules; nobody argues over the customer in the lobby.
- **Channel-accompanied customer**: verify the report and the showing eligibility first. Never re-classify to save a deal — the commission dispute that follows will cost more than the deal.
- **Customer only asks for the lowest price**: confirm the unit, the payment structure, and the need first — then price. "The lowest is X" as a reflex answer skips matching entirely and hands the negotiation away.
- **Customer is visibly tired or overloaded**: stop presenting. Play back three conclusions, arrange the departure or the next showing. Pushing past this point produces a nodding customer who signs nothing and returns for nothing.

## When is a first visit "valid"?

A valid first visit completes at least three things: **a needs profile the customer confirmed**, **one showing or comparison that actually matched the hard constraints**, and **one next action the customer agreed to**. No price calculated does not mean the visit failed — grading reception quality by whether a deposit changed hands is how teams teach their consultants to rush, and rushing is where mis-sold units come from.

**The closing line:**

> "Today we pinned down three things: what your household values most is {{top_priority}}, what you can't accept is {{deal_breaker}}, and the best things to compare next are {{units_or_options}}. I've kept the confirmed items and the to-be-verified items separately — the next step is {{next_step_and_when}}. If new questions come up when you talk it over at home, send me the question in your own words and I'll go check that specific thing — not send you a pile of ads."
